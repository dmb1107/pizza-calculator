# Contemporary Neapolitan — 65% Biga
### Grain Craft Neapolitan 00 · Ooni Halo Core · Gozney Tread · 265 g balls

---

## 1. What this recipe is

A contemporary Neapolitan dough built on a **stiff Italian biga carrying 65% of the flour**, finished at 70% hydration, baked at 750 °F on full flame.

**Why a biga.** Its low water activity suppresses protease mobility, so the gluten survives a long ferment instead of degrading, and it pushes the bacteria toward heterofermentative pathways — more acetic acid, a sharper and more complex aroma, and the big irregular alveoli that define the contemporary Neapolitan cornicione. It is also the more forgiving preferment: slower acid production and a wider usable window than a liquid preferment gives you.

**Why 65% and not 100%.** The flavor-versus-preferment-percentage curve flattens sharply above ~60%, while the risks keep climbing. Three reasons to stop at 65%:

- **Flour strength.** Every serious biga source calls for W 300+ / 12.5%+ protein for long ferments. Grain Craft Neapolitan is 12.2–12.8% protein — capable, but at the lower edge. Holding 35% of the flour out of the preferment leaves un-fermented gluten in the final dough as structural margin.
- **A live consistency lever.** The reserved water gets added by feel during the mix, so you can correct for a wetter or drier biga instead of committing everything up front.
- **Mixer load.** A smaller biga is easier to break down, and the breakdown phase is the hardest work the machine does.

Once this has run cleanly three or four times, pushing to 80% or 100% biga is a clean single-variable experiment.

## 2. Master formula

Baker's percentages on **total flour**:

| Component | % |
|---|---|
| Grain Craft Neapolitan 00 | 100 |
| Water (total) | 70 |
| Salt (Diamond Crystal kosher) | 2.8 |
| ADY | in biga only (see §5) |
| **Biga** | 65% of flour @ **50% hydration** |
| **Final mix** | 35% of flour + remaining water + all salt |

**Dough yield factor: 1.728** · Ball weight **265 g** · No malt, no oil, no sugar.

**Why these numbers:**
- **70% hydration** — enough to get an open, airy crumb and a puffy cornicione in a 60–90 second bake, without exceeding what a 12.5%-protein flour can hold through a long ferment. A biga dough handles drier than the number suggests, because the biga's gluten is already built before the water goes in.
- **2.8% salt** — at the upper end of the Neapolitan range (2.5–3.0%), which tightens the gluten slightly and slows fermentation a touch, both useful over a long schedule. Salt goes in the **final mix only** — never in the biga, where it would suppress the yeast you are trying to propagate.
- **50% biga hydration** — the documented band is **44–50%**. Giorilli codified 45% and allows up to 50% for less-refined flours; Grain Craft at 0.55% ash sits just outside true-00 refinement, and 50% hand-mixes more evenly. Now that the biga is hand-mixed, mixer load is no longer a reason to move it.
- **No diastatic malt.** In a 60–90 second bake on full flame, added sugars and extra amylase have nothing to do but burn. Grain Craft is unmalted, and that's correct here.

### Scaling to any batch — the arithmetic

The table above covers 3/6/9 at 265 g, and the split table below covers 12 and 18. For anything else — a different ball weight, 5 balls, 7 balls — run this. It's seven lines and it's all the recipe needs.

**Start:** `F = (balls × ball weight × 1.022) ÷ 1.728`

| | Formula | Check at 9 × 265 g |
|---|---|---|
| Total flour | `F` | 1410.6 g |
| Biga flour | `F × 0.65` | 916.9 g |
| Biga water | `F × 0.325` | 458.4 g |
| Biga ADY | `F × 0.0024375` | 3.44 g |
| Final-mix flour | `F × 0.35` | 493.7 g |
| Final-mix water | `F × 0.375` | 529.0 g |
| Salt | `F × 0.028` | 39.5 g |

Where the constants come from: **1.728** is the dough yield factor (1 + 0.70 water + 0.028 salt), **1.022** is the 2.2% overage. Biga water is 0.65 × 0.50; final water is 0.70 − 0.325; ADY is 0.65 × 0.00375.

⚠️ **Note the two `0.375`s are unrelated** — one is final-mix water as a fraction of total flour, the other is the ADY percentage of biga flour. They collide by coincidence.

**Then check both Halo Core limits before you mix:**

- Final dough `F × 1.728` ≤ **2500 g**, and `F` ≤ **1505 g** → otherwise split the final mix
- Biga flour `F × 0.65` ≤ **1610 g** → otherwise split the biga too

Every mixing speed and duration (§4, §8) holds at any batch size. **The water-temperature formula does not** — the mixer bowl is a fixed thermal mass while everything else scales with flour, so the weights shift with batch size (§6). Friction factor also changes with scale, which is why §6 keeps one per batch size.

### Bigger parties — the Halo Core forces a split

The Core takes **0.5–2.5 kg dough** and caps flour at **1505 g at 66%+ hydration**.

| Balls | Biga | Final mix |
|---|---|---|
| **12** | **One biga**: 1222.5 g flour + 611.2 g water + 4.58 g ADY (1833.7 g) — fits | Split biga into **2 × 916.9 g**, run **two 6-ball final mixes** |
| **18** | **Two bigas**, each the 9-ball spec (1375.3 g) — 1833.7 g of biga flour exceeds the 1610 g low-hydration cap | **Two 9-ball final mixes** |

⚠️ **A split batch needs its water temperature calculated per mix, not once.** The thermal system is the mix, and the bowl is in a different state for each — see §6. At 18 balls there is also only one mixer bowl for two bigas, so the second ferments elsewhere.

### Note on small batches — 3 balls is the floor

The biga is hand-mixed at every size (§5), so the Halo Core's 500 g minimum never applies to it — which is convenient, since the 3-ball biga would have been 458 g and under the limit.

The 3-ball **final dough** is 812.5 g, above the 500 g minimum but near it; expect some hook climb.

**Below 3 balls this stops being a mixer recipe.** Two balls is 542.5 g — it clears the 500 g floor on paper, but a spiral hook barely engages that little dough, and there's a second problem that isn't obvious: the mixer bowl is fixed mass, so as the batch shrinks the bowl becomes a larger share of the thermal system and the water has to carry more of the work. At 2 balls the required water temperature reaches **116 °F**, and at 1 ball **146 °F** — past what any domestic tap delivers. The arithmetic in this section still works if you want to scale down and mix by hand, but **3 balls is the smallest supported machine batch**, and the calculator enforces that.

## 4. Mixer reference

### Speed → RPM — MEASURED AND CONFIRMED

**Counted 20 hook revolutions in 20 s at 5% → 60 RPM.**

That settles it. The **Ooni help-center chart in the project files is wrong** — it claims a straight 3 × % line (5% = 15 RPM), which would have put 5% at 5 revolutions in 20 s. The press spec is correct: **60–300 RPM across 20 levels.**

> ### **RPM = 47.4 + 2.526 × dial%**

| Level | Dial | RPM | Used for |
|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 1 | 5% | **60** | floor — no slower setting exists |
| 2 | 10% | 73 | |
| 3 | **15%** | **85** | biga mix · Phase A breakdown |
| 4 | **20%** | **98** | Phase B bassinage · Phase D finish |
| 5 | 25% | 111 | |
| 6 | **30%** | **123** | Phase C development |
| 7 | 35% | 136 | |
| 8 | **40%** | **148** | hard ceiling for this dough |
| 10 | 50% | 174 | |
| 16 | 80% | 250 | Ooni max recommended @ 66%+ hyd |
| 20 | 100% | 300 | never with dough |

Each 5% step = **12.6 RPM**. Note the intercept: the dial maps across a *usable band*, not from zero. The Halo Pro works the same way (`RPM ≈ 45 + 3 × %`, from Ooni's own Pro recipes) — same design logic, different band. That cross-check corroborates the measurement.

**This validates the mix profile as written.** At real speeds the protocol lands squarely on Ooni's own professional Halo Pro recipes — their 100% biga mix runs at 100 RPM (mine: 85 for a stiffer 50% biga on a smaller machine), and their development speeds are 90–120 RPM (mine: 123). Total work is ~1,366 RPM-minutes vs ~1,200 for Ooni's Pro "Classic" dough. Right neighborhood, no changes needed.

**Two consequences that do change things:**

1. **The minimum is 60 RPM — there is no creep speed.** You cannot gently fold liquid in. Add water and flour with the mixer **off or at 5%**, then bring the dial up. Dumping water into dry flour at 85 RPM will throw flour out of the bowl, and adding bassinage water fast at 98 RPM will sling it.
2. **Friction runs hotter than the low reading would have implied** — see the revised FF starting guess in §6.

*Caveat: that count was on an empty bowl. Under load the hook will sag somewhat below the table, and motor protection deliberately reduces it further. Treat these as no-load figures.*

### Hard limits

- Dough: **0.5–2.5 kg** · Flour cap: **1505 g** @ 66%+ hyd, **1610 g** @ 55–59%
- Recommended speed: **5–80%** @ 60%+ hyd, **5–20%** @ 55–59% hyd
- **Max continuous run: 20 minutes**
- **Never run dough near 100%.** Ooni's manual is explicit about this.
- Motor protection auto-slows under load. **If it engages, stop, rest 5 min, resume at one step lower.** Log it — that's data about your friction factor.

## 5. Biga yeast, maturity, and mixing — per published practice

Everything in this section is codified Italian practice, not extrapolation. Sources at the end.

### Yeast: the Giorilli standard

> ### **1% fresh yeast = 0.30% IDY = 0.375% ADY, on biga flour**
> ### 12–18 h at **61–65 °F**, biga at **45–50% hydration**

This is the number Piergiorgio Giorilli codified and that essentially every serious source repeats — PizzaBlab, Gozney's own 100% biga recipe, Stadler Made, the Italian baking literature. It is a *baseline for 12–16 h at around 68 °F, or 16–18 h at 61–65 °F.* Go longer and you cut it; run warmer and you cut it.

| Balls | Biga flour | **ADY @ 0.375%** | (IDY @ 0.30%) |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 3 | 305.6 g | **1.15 g** | 0.92 g |
| 6 | 611.2 g | **2.29 g** | 1.83 g |
| 9 | 916.9 g | **3.44 g** | 2.75 g |
| 12 | 1222.5 g | **4.58 g** | 3.67 g |
| 18 | 1833.7 g | **6.88 g** | 5.50 g |

**Why 0.375% and not 0.38%.** The published anchor is **1% fresh yeast** — that is the number Giorilli states and the only one that is actually sourced. Everything downstream is unit conversion: fresh → IDY at 0.30, IDY → ADY at ×1.25, giving 0.375% exactly. Earlier drafts printed the rounded 0.38% in the prose while computing the tables at 0.375%, which made the document contradict itself by about 1.3% of the yeast. That difference is far inside weighing and ripeness-judgement noise and would never show up in a bake — it is fixed here because a recipe that disagrees with its own arithmetic can't be checked, not because the dough cared.

**For a temperature or duration outside the baseline, use [PizzaBlab's Dough Calculator](https://www.pizzablab.com/calculators/pizza-dough-calculator/) rather than a table from me.** It's built for exactly this and it's the same source the rest of this section comes from.

**Note the yeast is dissolved in room-temperature water, not warm and not cold.** PizzaBlab is explicit that cold water damages yeast cells; there's no proofing/activation step in the classic method. At 1.15–4.58 g you're well clear of scale resolution, so no slurry gymnastics needed either.

### Ripeness: a biga does NOT double

**A ripe biga puffs up roughly 20%. That's it.**

The chunks look slightly swollen and may knit into a loose cohesive block. The smell is moderately sharp, alcoholic-sour, and *mild* — not overpowering.

- **Doubled in size** → it was **over-mixed** (you developed gluten, so it rose like a dough instead of fermenting like a biga)
- **Strong, sharp acidic/alcoholic smell** → over-fermented
- **No puffing at all** → not ready

The window is genuinely wide. Slower fermentation, slower acid production and less gluten breakdown mean a biga is hard to ruin by an hour either way — plan to hit the cue, not the clock.

### Mixing: by hand, and mix — don't knead

**Hand-mix the biga at every batch size.** This isn't a concession for small batches; it's the method. Gozney's 100% biga recipe says to mix by hand to a dry lumpy consistency, and PizzaBlab warns specifically against forming a cohesive mass.

The goal is **small-to-medium chunks, like gnocchi** — not a dough. A spiral mixer's whole purpose is building a gluten network, which is precisely what you don't want here, and it's what causes the over-mixed biga that then doubles and misleads you on ripeness.

**Mix and ferment the biga in the mixer bowl.** Not a separate tub — the same 965 g stainless bowl the final mix runs in. This is a standing decision, not a per-bake choice, and it has two consequences worth stating up front: the bowl arrives at the final mix cold rather than at room temperature, and it is tied up for the whole ~21-hour biga schedule. Both are accounted for in §6.

Method: water and yeast into the mixer bowl, mix to dissolve. Add flour. Make a claw with your hand and circulate fingertips through it. **3–6 minutes, until no dry flour remains** — any dry flour never ferments. Break up any large chunks by hand.

Cover to prevent drying. Sources differ on venting (Gozney and Ooni say leave a gap; PizzaBlab says it serves no purpose) — either is fine, the thing that matters is that it doesn't dry out.

*This removes the Halo Core capacity problem entirely — no batch size is too small or too stiff for the biga phase any more.*

### Why 61–65 °F specifically

It isn't just about speed. That range produces the **right ratio of lactic to acetic acid**, which is what gives biga its characteristic sharp, vinegary profile. Ferment much warmer and you get a preferment that is biga-shaped but tastes different.

**This is why your unstable kitchen is a real problem, not just a timing nuisance** — and why the fridge-retarded schedule in §7 is the answer.

## 6. DDT, friction factor, and water temperature

### Target DDT

**75 °F** for **3–6 balls** · **74 °F** for **7 or more**

A 65% biga delivers a large, already-active yeast population into the final mix, and this dough goes straight into the fridge. Every degree of starting temperature buys extra uncontrolled fermentation during the hours it takes the mass to cool, so the target sits at the cool end of the 73–77 °F Neapolitan band. Bigger batches cool slower, hence the extra degree down at 7 and above.

### The water temperature formula

Standard "multiply DDT by 4" arithmetic breaks down here — it weights the preferment as one of four equal factors, but the biga is **56% of the final dough mass.** So this uses a mass-and-specific-heat weighted mix.

**And it must include the mixer bowl.** ⚠️ *Revised after bake 1 — the earlier bowl-free version was wrong by 5 °F on water temperature.*

Compute each component's heat capacity (`mass × specific heat`):

| Component | Specific heat |
|---|---:|
| Biga @ 50% hydration | 0.6133 |
| Fresh flour | 0.42 |
| Water | 1.00 |
| Salt | 0.21 |
| **Stainless bowl (965 g → C = 115.8)** | **0.12** |

Let `Ct` = the four dough components summed, and `C_bowl` = 115.8.

> ### **T_water = [ DDT × (Ct + C_bowl) − FF × Ct − Cb·T_biga − Cf·T_flour − Cs·T_room − C_bowl·T_bowl ] ÷ Cw**

**`T_bowl` defaults to `T_biga`,** since the biga always ferments in the mixer bowl (§5) and 19 hours of contact puts them at equilibrium.

⚠️ **But that equilibrium does not survive the last ten minutes before mixing, and bake 1 says so.** The biga read **53 °F at pull and 58 °F after being broken apart** — five degrees gained from handling and air exposure, which the bowl did not share. The corroborating evidence is in the same log: the Phase C friction rate *climbed* from 0.82 to 1.00 °F/min during the mix, which is what a bowl playing catch-up looks like.

**So measure the bowl.** One reading against the bowl wall at mix start, five seconds. The equilibrium argument was sound about the fermentation and wrong about the tearing, and the gap is worth 3.3 °F of water at 3 balls (§6, sensitivity table below).

**⚠️ FF is defined as the rise the mixer produces in the dough *alone*.** That's why the work term is `FF × Ct` and not `FF × (Ct + C_bowl)`. Get this wrong and the formula silently returns a water temperature several degrees off.

**This is no longer scale-independent.** The old `3.00 ×` shortcut is dead — the bowl is fixed mass while everything else scales with flour, so the weights shift with batch size. Always compute from component masses.

### Why the bowl matters

Two separate effects, and only one of them is significant:

**Its temperature — negligible.** The sensitivity is `C_bowl / (Ct + C_bowl)`:

| Batch | °F of dough per 1 °F of bowl-temp error |
|---|---:|
| 3 balls | 0.18 |
| 6 balls | 0.10 |
| 9 balls | 0.07 |

At 6 balls, being 3 °F off on the bowl costs 0.3 °F in the dough; a 20 °F cold bowl versus a room-temp one costs 2.0 °F. Both figures come from the same coefficient — quote it rather than either endpoint.

**But how much the bowl *moves the answer* is a different question from how much a misjudgement costs.** "How wrong is my dough if I misjudge the bowl" is `C_bowl / (Ct + C_bowl)`. "How much does the water target shift per degree of bowl" is `C_bowl / Cw`, which is three times larger because water is only 30% of the system:

| Batch | °F of dough per °F of bowl | °F of water per °F of bowl |
|---|---:|---:|
| 3 balls | 0.18 | **0.66** |
| 6 balls | 0.10 | 0.33 |
| 9 balls | 0.07 | 0.22 |
| 18 balls | 0.04 | 0.11 |

**The second column is why the bowl is worth measuring and the first is why it was easy to dismiss.** A 5 °F bowl error barely moves the dough — 0.9 °F at 3 balls — but it moves the water target 3.3 °F, and the water target is the number you act on.

**Since the bowl always ferments the biga (§5), there is only one lever on it: the temper.** An hour on the counter warms bowl and biga together and pulls the whole cold end of the system up. Skipping it is the single most expensive shortcut in the schedule — each degree of biga temperature is worth about 2 °F of water, and at 3 balls a skipped temper is what pushes the requirement toward 100 °F.

### Split batches: the thermal system is the mix, not the batch

⚠️ **A 12-ball batch is not a 12-ball thermal system. It is a 6-ball one, twice.** The bowl faces one mix at a time, so every heat capacity in the formula is computed from **per-mix** masses. Computing from batch totals halves the bowl's apparent share and lands the water target **2.6 °F low at 12 balls, 1.8 °F low at 18.** Same category of error as leaving the bowl out entirely, just smaller.

`FF` is per-mix for the same reason — it is the rise the mixer puts into the dough that is actually in the bowl, and 14.04 was measured on a single 6-ball mix.

**And the bowl is in a different state for each mix.** Three states, and each takes its value from something already measured:

| Bowl state | Take `T_bowl` from | When |
|---|---|---|
| **Cold — held the biga** | biga temperature | Mix 1, and every single-mix batch |
| **Room temperature** | room temperature | Bowl washed and left out; a second biga fermented elsewhere |
| **Warm from the previous mix** | DDT | Mix 2 onward — *a ceiling, not an estimate* |

At 12 balls that is a 5.3 °F spread in the water target between mix 1 and mix 2 (64.8 °F then 59.5 °F). Real, and worth getting right.

**Rinsing is the lever.** The bowl is thin stainless — running water resets it to roughly the rinse temperature inside a minute. If mix 2's number comes out awkward, set the bowl deliberately rather than chasing it with the water.

**Re-measure both the bowl and the biga before mix 2.** They drift in opposite directions while mix 1 runs — bowl up toward DDT, waiting biga up toward the room — and **the biga is the bigger term:** −1.59 °F of water per °F of biga against −0.33 for the bowl. Neither drift is modelled here and neither should be; there is no data for it. Two readings, thirty seconds, recompute.

**Its mass — this is the real one.** Friction energy heats whatever is in the bowl, and the bowl is part of "whatever." Same work, more mass, less temperature rise:

| Balls **per mix** | Bowl share | Rise diluted to | FF 14 appears as |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 3 | 17.9% | 82% | 11.5 °F |
| 6 | 9.9% | 90% | 12.6 °F |
| 9 | 6.8% | 93% | 13.0 °F |

⚠️ **Keyed on mix size, not batch size.** Earlier drafts carried 12- and 18-ball rows at 5.2% and 3.5%, computed against batch totals. Those describe thermal systems this recipe never builds: 12 balls runs as two 6-ball mixes and reads off the 6 row, 18 balls as two 9-ball mixes and reads off the 9. The floor of the bowl's share is about **6.6%**, set by the mixer's 2500 g capacity rather than by any batch size: Ct runs 0.6516 per gram of dough, so at the cap the share is 115.8 / (0.6516 × 2500 + 115.8) = 6.64%. Nothing gets below it because nothing puts more than 2500 g against the bowl — and note a *split* batch can come closer to the cap than any unsplit one, since 19 × 257 g runs as two 2495 g mixes against 9 × 270 g's 2483 g.

**This is exactly why folding the bowl into FF fails.** The apparent FF would drift from 11.5 to 13.0 across the range for no physical reason — you'd conclude the mixer behaves differently at different scales when really one constant is being read through three different dilutions.

### Friction factor — MEASURED

> ### **FF = 14.0 °F** (bake 1, 6 balls)

`FF = [ T_final × (Ct + C_bowl) − Cb·T_biga − Cf·T_flour − Cw·T_water − Cs·T_room − C_bowl·T_bowl ] ÷ Ct`

Bake 1 gave 14.04 °F, corroborated independently by the Phase C friction rate (1.00 °F/min observed on dough+bowl = 1.11 °F/min dough-only, against 1.08 predicted).

**Still one data point.** The falsifiable test: if the bowl model is right, FF stays near 14 at 3 and 9 balls while the *raw temperature rise* looks quite different (11.5 vs 13.0). If FF drifts even after the dilution correction, something else is going on.

**Three things that still apply:**

- **FF is a property of the profile, not the machine.** Change speeds or times and it moves. Roughly +1 °F per additional minute at 30%.
- **FF still varies with batch size** on top of the bowl dilution — more total work, less surface area per unit mass to shed it. Keep a separate value per size.
- **Heat of hydration is already included.** Flour releases 1.5–3 °F of exotherm as it absorbs water, during the mix, so it's inside the measured final temperature and inside FF. It is one combined number.

### Hitting the water temperature

**Blend fridge-cold water with tap water until the thermometer reads the target.** No arithmetic, no ice, no measuring the split — pour, stir, read, adjust. Water is the one ingredient you can check before you commit it.

**Fridge water covers roughly 38–60 °F. Tap and warmer covers the rest.** On the retarded-biga schedule the biga arrives at 45–60 °F, and across the supported range — 3 to 24 balls, 240–300 g each, kitchens from 60 to 84 °F — the required water spans **53 °F to 109 °F.** At the default 265 g ball it is **53–107 °F.**

⚠️ **The old "52–90 °F" figure in earlier drafts was wrong at the top**, because the sweep behind it never went below about 9 balls. The requirement climbs as the **mix** gets smaller, since the fixed-mass bowl is a bigger share of a small system:

| Balls | 3 | 6 | 9 | 12 | 18 | 24 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Balls per mix | 3 | 6 | 9 | **6** | **9** | **8** |
| Hottest water asked for (°F) | **106.6** | 96.8 | 90.3 | **93.4** | 90.3 | 91.1 |

**Read that second row, not the first.** A 12-ball batch runs as two 6-ball mixes, so it wants *hotter* water than a 9-ball batch does — the number tracks the mix, not the batch, and it does not fall smoothly as the batch grows.

The cold end works the same way in reverse: the coldest the model ever asks for is **53 °F**, set by the largest single mix that fits the machine (9 × 270 g). A 24-ball batch does not go colder, because it is three 8-ball mixes rather than one enormous one.

All of it is reachable from a hot tap, which is why 3 balls is the supported floor rather than 1 or 2 (§3).

Three directions to be ready for:

- **Cold biga (the usual case)** — the biga is the dominant thermal term, so the colder it is, the *warmer* the water needs to be. A biga straight from the fridge can call for water in the 80s or 90s.
- **Warm kitchen, warm biga** — fridge water, possibly most of the charge.
- **Above ~120 °F, stop and fix it upstream.** No domestic tap gets there and heating a portion in a kettle is a sign the inputs are wrong, not that the water needs to be hotter. The cause is almost always a biga that skipped its 1-hour temper. Give it the hour; each degree of biga temperature is worth about 2 °F of water.

**Get the water to temperature *before* it goes in.** Don't try to correct a blend once it's in the bowl.

<details>
<summary>Ice — emergency use only</summary>

You should never need it. Sub-38 °F water is only reachable on the classic room-temperature biga track in a hot kitchen; on the retarded schedule the coldest the model ever asks for is about 52 °F.

If you do end up there: melting ice absorbs 80 cal/g with no temperature change, which is the same energy as cooling that gram of water by 144 °F. So ice behaves like water at `−112 − 0.5 × (32 − T_ice)` — your 16 °F freezer gives −120 °F. It's a bookkeeping trick, not a real temperature.

Keep it under ~35% of the water and make sure every gram melts before you take a reading, or the dough reads on target and then drifts cold.

</details>

### Measuring your friction factor

**14.0 °F is measured, not assumed** — see above. For context on whether that's plausible: commercial spirals typically land 20–26 °F on a full bread mix, and this is a shorter profile on a smaller machine with a 10-minute rest in the middle, so the low end of that range is where it should sit.

Protocol:
1. Record every input mass and temperature.
2. Run the mix profile in §8 exactly.
3. Probe the dough **immediately** at the end — three spots, center of the mass, average them.
4. `FF = measured − predicted`. Use it next time.

**Three things that will bite you:**

- **FF is a property of the profile, not the machine.** Change speeds or times and it moves. Rough scaling: **+1 °F per additional minute at 30%** (0.75 °F/min at 15%, 0.86 at 20%). Re-measure whenever you change the routine.
- ⚠️ **Those per-minute rates are dough-only, like FF itself.** A thermometer reads the dough after it has equilibrated with the bowl, so what you *observe* is the rate times `Ct/(Ct + C_bowl)` — 0.82 at 3 balls, 0.90 at 6, 0.93 at 9. Multiply before you compare a rate to a measurement. Conflating the two conventions is what produced the old "probe at DDT − 4" rule, which was over a degree wrong at small batches.
- **FF differs by batch size.** A 9-ball batch runs a higher FF than a 3-ball batch — more total work done, less surface area per unit mass to shed it. **Keep a separate FF for 3-ball, 6-ball and 9-ball.** Expect the 3-ball to come in several degrees lower.
- **Heat of hydration is already included — don't add it again.** Flour releases roughly 1.5–3 °F of exothermic heat as it absorbs water. You don't need to model that separately: it happens during the mix, so it's already inside the final temperature you measured, and therefore already inside your FF. FF as measured here is a single combined number covering mixer friction *and* hydration exotherm. If you ever see a calculator asking for a "friction factor" alongside a separate hydration correction, that's a different convention — don't feed it this number.

## 7. Schedules

### The architecture problem, stated plainly

Classic biga **front-loads the entire fermentation**. At 0.375% ADY on 65% biga flour you carry **0.244% ADY on total flour** — a heavy dose by pizza standards, and deliberately so, because the preferment is meant to do essentially all the work. Every documented biga recipe then gives the final dough a *short* proof: Giorilli and Gozney a few hours, Ooni 2 h at room temperature or 6–36 h in the fridge.

This is the opposite of a lightly-prefermented dough that gets its character from days in the fridge. **Stack a full-strength classic biga on top of a 50-hour cold ferment and you have specified two complete fermentations.** The dough will blow out.

Second thing worth absorbing: **in Italian practice you get more time by lengthening the biga, not the ball proof.** PizzaBlab's range is 12–24 h; "biga lunga" runs 24 h at 39 °F then 24 h at room temperature. The length lives in the preferment.

### Recommended: the Ooni / Marco Fuso retarded biga

Published professional practice, and the fridge holds the biga at a stable temperature instead of leaving it to whatever the kitchen is doing.

| Stage | Time | Temp | Step |
|---|---|---|---|
| Biga, room temperature | 2 h | ambient | §8.1 |
| Biga, refrigerated | 18–20 h | ~39 °F | §8.1 |
| Biga rest before mixing | 1 h | ambient | §8.2 |
| Final mix | ~30 min | DDT 74–75 °F | §8.2 |
| Bulk rest | 45–60 min | ambient | §8.3 step 7 |
| Divide and ball | 15–20 min | ambient | §8.3 step 8 |
| Balls at room temperature | **set by final dough temp** (71–144 min) | ambient | §8.3 step 9 |
| Dough, refrigerated | **6–36 h** | 38–40 °F | §8.3 step 10 |
| Temper | 2–3 h | ambient | §8.4 step 11 |

**Total: ~34 h (6 h cold) · ~52 h (24 h cold) · ~64 h (36 h cold)**

Those are the defaults. Everything except the cold ferment is fixed overhead: **27.8 h at the defaults, 25.6–30.8 h across the full input ranges** — so the arithmetic is `cold ferment + ~28 h`.

**A split batch runs 28.1 h of overhead**, not 27.8. The second mix and changeover add 0.58 h, and the stagger correction below takes 0.29 h back off the ball rise — so the net is +0.29 h, not +0.58. (Three mixes, which only happens at 24 balls, comes to 28.4 h.) The 5-minute changeover assumes the bowl is *not* cleaned and every mix is weighed out before the first one starts.

### Split batches run on one clock, and one dough is ahead of it

Both doughs go into **one bulk container**, so the batch cools as a single mass — which is why `DDT` stays keyed to total balls (74 at 12, not the 75 a lone 6-ball mix would get). The cost is scheduling.

**Mix 1's dough finishes 35 minutes before mix 2's.** Once they are in the same tub they are indistinguishable, so you cannot give the halves separate clocks.

- **Clock the bulk rest from the second mix.** It is the only anchor that gives mix 2 any bulk at all. Mix 1's half runs long by design.
- **Then take 17½ minutes off the ball room-temperature rise** — half the stagger. At 12 or 18 balls that turns a 90-minute rise into **72½**.

⚠️ **This centres the error rather than removing it.** Before the correction mix 1's half is 35 minutes over and mix 2's is exactly on time; after it, they are +17½ and −17½. One clock cannot do better than that, but halving the worst case is worth twenty seconds of arithmetic.

⚠️ **And on a warm dough it may not centre at all.** The ball rise has a 45-minute floor, so when the dough is warm enough that the computed rise is already near it, there is nothing left to subtract. At a 74 °F DDT a 77 °F dough computes a 62-minute rise, which clamps at 45 after a two-mix correction and stays at 45 after a three-mix one — leaving 18 minutes uncorrected. This bites hardest where it matters most, since a warm dough ferments fastest. The floor is not worth overruling for it; the levers are upstream, in mix count and dough temperature.

**Don't clean the bowl between mixes.** It costs changeover time and buys nothing:

- **The residue is thermally neutral.** It is already sitting at DDT, so it contributes its own share to the heat balance. The required water for mix 2 is identical at 0 g, 30 g or 60 g of carry-over — not approximately, exactly.
- **The yield cancels.** Residue transfers forward: mix 1 loses it, mix 2 gains it, and both land in the same tub. Only what stays in the bowl after the *last* mix is a real loss, and that is what the 2.2% overage was always for.

Rinsing is still a lever if you want one — thin stainless resets to roughly the rinse temperature in under a minute, so you can set the bowl deliberately if mix 2's water target comes out awkward. It just isn't worth doing by default.

This is derived, not measured. It assumes fermentation during bulk and during the ball rise are worth the same at the same temperature — same dough, same temperature, and dividing displaces gas without resetting fermentation, so it should hold. It has not been tested.

The 36 h end lands at ~64 h total with every number still inside published guidance. Start at **24 h cold (~52 h total)** and work up.

### Alternative: classic room-temperature biga

If you can hold **61–65 °F** reliably — wine fridge, cool basement, winter — run the codified version: **16–18 h at 61–65 °F**, then a short final proof. Total ~24–30 h. This produces the truest biga acid profile, because that temperature band is doing flavor work the fridge cannot replicate.

Your Home Assistant sensors would tell you quickly whether anywhere in the house actually holds that band.

### Going past 64 h

Beyond 36 h of cold ferment you are off documented ground and into your own calibration. Two principled routes, in order of preference:

1. **Lengthen the biga toward 24 h** (PizzaBlab's stated ceiling) with correspondingly less yeast per their calculator, keeping the dough's cold ferment moderate.
2. **Keep the schedule and cut the biga yeast** — but this is no longer the codified method, and it is the change most likely to give you a weak, under-developed biga rather than a longer one.

Do neither until the ~50 h version has worked twice.

## 8. Procedure

### Stage 1 — Biga (hand-mixed)

1. Weigh biga flour, then **break up the clumps while it's still dry** — whisk hard or push it through a coarse sieve. Weigh first, then break up, so nothing lost changes the number.
   *Grain Craft arrives lumpy; it's a milling artifact, not a quality problem. Dry is the only easy time to fix it. A clump that survives into the biga is dry flour at its core that never ferments, and in a stiff 50% biga you will not find it by hand.*
2. Weigh biga water — **room temperature, not cold, not warm.**
3. Water and ADY **into the mixer bowl** — the biga ferments in it (§5). Stir to dissolve. Add the flour.
4. **Mix by hand, 3–6 min.** Claw shape, fingertips circulating. **Mix, do not knead.**
   **Target: small-to-medium gnocchi-sized chunks, no dry flour anywhere.** It should look like crumbs, not dough. Break up large chunks by hand.
   *Do not use the mixer.* A spiral hook builds gluten, which is the one thing you do not want here.
5. Cover so it cannot dry out. Ferment per your track in §7.
6. **Pull at ~20% volume increase** — puffed chunks, mild alcoholic-sour smell. Not doubled.

### Stage 2 — Final mix (~15 min run time)

Have everything weighed before you start, and the water already blended to temperature.

| Phase | Speed | Time | What |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| **Prep** | — | — | Break up clumps in the fresh flour dry, same as the biga flour. Crumble the biga small — smaller is better, it is the stiffest thing the mixer will face. Add the flour, toss to coat. |
| **A — breakdown** | **15%** / 85 RPM | 3–4 min | Add **60% of the final water — weigh it** (3 balls 105.8 g · 6 balls 211.6 g · 9 balls 317.4 g) **with the mixer off**, then bring the dial up. Run until the biga pieces disappear into a rough shaggy mass. Highest-torque phase. *Optional (PizzaBlab): soak the crumbled biga in that water a few minutes first — but only a few. Working biga in water alone strips starch off the chunks and leaves hard, sticky gluten lumps.* |
| **B — bassinage** | **20%** / 98 RPM | 5–6 min | Add salt. Then the remaining 40% (3 balls 70.5 g · 6 balls 141.1 g · 9 balls 211.6 g) in **3 additions**, waiting for each to fully absorb before the next. Pour **slowly down the splash-guard spout** — at 98 RPM the hook will sling it if you dump it. |
| **— probe —** | 0% | — | **Target: 3 balls 72.2 °F · 6 balls 71.8 °F · 9 balls 70.5 °F** (at FF 14 in a 70 °F room). You are *not* aiming at DDT yet; about a third of the friction is still ahead of you. General form below. |
| **C — development** | **30%** / 123 RPM | 3–4 min | Runs to smooth and glossy. Adjust the duration from the probe: **~1 °F per minute** at 6 balls or larger, **~0.9 at 3 balls**. |
| **Rest** | 0% | 10 min | Bowl covered, mixer off. Relaxes gluten; the dough will smooth out on its own. |
| **D — finish** | **20%** / 98 RPM | 45–60 s | Final smoothing. Dough should pull cleanly off the bowl wall. |

**Never above 40% (148 RPM) with this dough.** Total run ~15 min, inside the 20-minute continuous limit, and the rest breaks it up anyway.

#### Reading the probe

**Why below DDT and not at it.** At the end of Phase B you have absorbed roughly two thirds of the total friction — Phases A and B are long, and the hydration exotherm has already fired.

Still to come, **stated as the probe will read it** (dough and bowl equilibrated, 6 balls): Phase C **+3.4 °F**, Phase D **+0.8 °F**, minus **1.0 °F** given back to the room during the 10-minute rest. Net **+3.2 °F.**

⚠️ **There is no flat "DDT − 4" rule, and earlier drafts were wrong to give one.** The gap shrinks with batch size, because a small batch has proportionally more bowl to heat:

| Balls | 3 | 6 | 9 | 12 | 18 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Balls per mix | 3 | 6 | 9 | **6** | **9** |
| Probe target | **DDT − 2.8** | **DDT − 3.2** | **DDT − 3.5** | **DDT − 3.4** | **DDT − 3.5** |

18 balls is two 9-ball mixes, so its target is identical to the 9-ball one — that equality is a useful check on any calculation claiming to be per-mix. 12 and 6 share a mix size but differ slightly because DDT is 74 at 12 balls and 75 at 6.

The old rule was 1.2 °F off at 3 balls — a third of Phase C's entire correction budget, spent in the wrong direction before you started. It came from taking the dough-only friction rates and reading them as if they were what a thermometer shows.

**The general form, once you know your own FF:**

> **Probe target = DDT − 0.33 × FF × Ct/(Ct + C_bowl) + 0.2 × (DDT − T_room)**

Two refinements from bake 1: the friction still to come is diluted by the bowl, and the rest loses heat in proportion to the dough-to-room gap rather than a flat 1 °F.

At FF 14 in a 70 °F room: **3 balls → 72.2 °F · 6 balls → 71.8 °F · 9 balls → 70.5 °F**

The 0.33 assumes nominal phase durations. Run Phase A or B long and more of the friction is already banked, so the probe should read higher.

**Acting on the reading** (nominal Phase C is 3.5 min):

| Probe reads | Do |
|---|---|
| **Target ±1 °F** | Run Phase C as written |
| **1–2 °F high** | Cut Phase C to 2–2.5 min |
| **1–2 °F low** | Extend Phase C to 4.5–5.5 min |
| **More than 2 °F off** | Accept the miss — fix the water temperature next batch |

**Phase C has limited authority, and this is the important part.** At 6 balls, cutting it to 2 min saves only **1.5 °F** and stretching it to 5.5 min adds only **2.0 °F** — and it is narrower at 3 balls (−1.3 / +1.8), wider at 9 (−1.5 / +2.0). Outside that window you are trading gluten development for temperature and losing both — an under-mixed dough at the right temperature is worse than a properly developed one that is 2 °F warm. Temperature misses get fixed upstream in the water calculation, not downstream by mangling the mix.

**Friction per minute at each speed**, if you need to correct in a different phase: 15% ≈ 0.75 °F/min · 20% ≈ 0.86 °F/min · 30% ≈ 1.08 °F/min. **These are dough-only.** What a thermometer shows is those numbers × `Ct/(Ct + C_bowl)` — 0.82 at 3 balls, 0.90 at 6, 0.93 at 9. At 30% that works out to an observed 0.89 / 0.97 / 1.01 °F per minute, which is where the "about a degree a minute" rule of thumb comes from.

**Done when:** smooth, glossy, "pumpkin-lattice" surface, cleans the bowl, thin windowpane with only slight tearing, **and at DDT ±1 °F.** Temperature is a pass/fail gate, not a suggestion — record the actual number every time.

### Stage 3 — Bulk, ball, cold

7. Turn out into a lightly oiled container. Rest **45–60 min** at room temp.
   **No folds.** The mixer has already built the gluten network, and the biga contributed a developed one before that. Folding now only tightens the dough further and costs extensibility.
8. Divide to **265 g**. Pre-round, rest 10–15 min, then ball tight.
9. Onto **very lightly oiled** half-sheet trays with lids — a thin film wiped out with a paper towel, not a pool. Nothing on top of the balls. **Room temp per the table below** — set by the dough temperature you actually hit, not a fixed number — then refrigerate.

   *Oil, not flour — see the note below.*
   **Adjust the room-temperature time to your actual dough temperature** (see below) rather than using a flat 90 min.

| Final dough | Room time |
|---:|---:|
| 77 °F | 71 min |
| 76 °F | 80 min |
| **75 °F (on target)** | **90 min** |
| 74 °F | 100 min |
| 73 °F | 110 min |
| 72 °F | 121 min |
| 71 °F | 133 min |
| 70 °F | 144 min |

`R' = (90 + 150)/f − 150` where `f = 2^((T_actual − DDT)/17)`. The 150 is the cooldown's equivalent fermentation at DDT — a cooler dough loses ground both on the counter *and* on the way down to 40 °F, and this compensates for both. Clamp to 45–180 min.

10. **Spread the trays out in the fridge for the first 4 h — do not stack.** A 265 g ball takes 3–4 h to reach 40 °F, and that whole window is warm fermentation you didn't budget for. Stacked trays can double it.

#### Why oil the trays, not flour

**Flour is the wrong release agent here.** It is hygroscopic — it pulls water out of the dough surface and hydrates into paste. Over 24–36 hours in a fridge, which is a drying environment even under a lid, you get the worst of both: patches of gluey paste where the flour hydrated, and a dry skin everywhere else. That skin resists opening and tears at the cornicione instead of stretching.

The traditional flour dusting comes from **wooden** dough boxes, which breathe and buffer moisture. Aluminum trays do neither — flour that lands on aluminum has nowhere to go but into the dough.

**Oil does the one job you need.** It is a barrier rather than an absorbent: it stops the dough bonding to the metal without taking any water out of it, and it prevents skinning over a long cold ferment.

**Separate the two jobs:**

| Job | Use |
|---|---|
| Release from the **tray** | thin oil film |
| Release from the **peel** | flour or semolina, at the bench, right before launch |

**Keep it to a film.** Wipe the tray with an oiled towel; you want the sheen, not a puddle. Too much oil and three things go wrong: the ball slides instead of gripping enough to hold its dome as it relaxes, the base picks up enough oil to fry and over-brown on the stone, and excess smokes on contact. A neutral oil is marginally better than olive here purely on smoke point, though at these quantities it barely matters.

**Nothing on top of the balls.** The lid handles humidity. Oil on the upper surface becomes the cornicione surface and darkens it unevenly.

### Stage 4 — Temper and bake

11. Out of the fridge **2–3 h** before baking. Target **60–65 °F at the core** — measure, don't guess. Under 55 °F and the dough tears on opening and won't spring; over 70 °F and it goes slack and sticky.
12. Ready when the balls have relaxed and spread slightly, domed, and feel airy with a slow, incomplete rebound when poked.

## 9. Baking on the Tread

**Keep doing exactly what already works: preheat until the gauge reads 750 °F, launch on full flame, 60–90 s.** Don't change the oven and the dough in the same bake — preferment, mixer and schedule are already three new variables.

- **Turn every 15–20 s.** Lateral flame plus a small chamber means a static face scorches fast.
- **265 g opens to 11.5–12"** — thickness factor 0.083 oz/in², squarely in the classic Neapolitan band. For a fatter cornicione against the Tread's 12" ceiling, open to 11" instead.
- **Recovery between pies.** The Tread heats and cools fast, which is the price of low thermal mass. Across 9–18 pizzas the stone is the limiting variable, not the dough — let the gauge come back to 750 rather than launching on a dropping stone.

### Why 750 + full flame is the correct call, not a compromise

Neapolitan baking is governed by the **ratio of top heat to bottom heat**, not by absolute temperature. The stone cooks the base by conduction; the flame cooks the top by radiation. If the base finishes before the top, you need a *larger* top-to-bottom ratio — which means lowering the stone, raising the flame, or both.

A 750 °F stone with full flame does exactly that. Pushing the stone to 800 °F+ moves the ratio the wrong way and burns the base before the cornicione has set. Your result is the predictable one.

*(One thing worth logging: the built-in gauge and an IR reading of the stone surface are different measurements and won't agree. If you ever gun the stone at gauge-750, write down what it says — that's the number that transfers to any other oven.)*

### The burn ring at the base of the cornicione

That specific pattern is diagnostic, and it is only partly about temperature.

The ring where the cornicione meets the flat center is the worst spot on the pizza for base scorching, for two reasons that stack:

1. **No moisture buffer.** Sauce and cheese hold the center near 100 °C by evaporative cooling until the water is gone. Sauce normally stops 1–1.5 cm short of the rim, so that ring gets full conductive heat with nothing above it absorbing energy.
2. **It's often the thinnest part of the base.** Pressing hard just inside the rim to define the cornicione thins the dough exactly there. Less mass, less thermal buffer, first to burn.

So it burns first — driest contact zone *and* thinnest cross-section, right on top of each other.

**Fixes, cheapest first:**

| Fix | Why |
|---|---|
| **Take the sauce closer to the rim** — leave ~1 cm, not 2 | Shrinks the unprotected ring, adds evaporative cooling where you're burning |
| **Open with a gradual thickness gradient** | Push out from the center with fingertips; don't press a hard groove at the rim |
| **Brush excess flour off the base before launch** | Loose flour and semolina carbonize fast and read as scorching even when the crust is fine |
| **Turn sooner — first turn at 15 s** | Any face that dwells over one spot runs ahead |
| **Lift and dome for 5–10 s** if the base is ahead | Takes the pie off conduction while radiation finishes the top |

If it persists after those, drop the launch temperature another 15–25 °F rather than shortening the bake — a shorter bake leaves the crumb gummy.

### If the new dough behaves differently

A long-fermented dough has fewer residual sugars, so it can bake **paler** than you're used to at the same settings. That's sugar depletion, not underbaking.

- **Pale but cooked through** → shorten the cold ferment before you touch the oven
- **Base ahead of the top** → the ring problem above; lower stone or more flame
- **Top ahead of the base** → the only case for nudging the stone up

Never fix color with a longer bake. It dries the crumb and costs you the open, custardy interior the biga is there to produce.

## 10. First-bake calibration

Run **6 balls on the Ooni/Fuso schedule with 24 h of dough cold ferment (~50 h total).** Not 3 (too small to characterize FF), not 9 (don't stress a new motor while you're still learning it). Log:

- [x] ~~Hook revolution count at 5%~~ → **DONE: 20 revs / 20 s = 60 RPM. `RPM = 47.4 + 2.526 × dial%`**
- [ ] Biga: ADY %, water temp, hours at RT vs fridge, and **volume gain at pull (target ~20%)**
- [ ] Every input mass and temperature at final mix
- [ ] Water temp used
- [ ] Dough temp after Phase B, and again at the end
- [ ] **FF = measured − predicted** ← the number this whole bake exists to produce
- [ ] Whether motor protection engaged, and in which phase
- [ ] Ball temp entering fridge, and at 4 h
- [ ] Ball core temp at launch
- [ ] Stone temp per pizza, bake time, crumb and cornicione notes

**Second bake:** same everything, with your real FF substituted. If DDT lands within ±1 °F, the model is calibrated and you can scale to 9 and 18 with confidence.

**Then, one variable at a time:** 80% biga → 100% biga → 72% hydration. In that order. Each is a clean single-variable test once the thermal model is locked.

## 11. Troubleshooting

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Biga **doubled** in volume | **Over-mixed** — you built gluten, so it rose like a dough | Mix by hand only, shorter, to loose chunks. Not a yeast problem. |
| Biga smells sharply acidic/alcoholic | Over-fermented | Shorten, or run the retarded schedule |
| Biga did not puff at all | Not ready, ambient colder than measured, or dead ADY | Give it longer. Probe actual ambient. Check the yeast. |
| Dry flour left in the biga | Under-mixed | Mix the full 3–6 min; dry flour never ferments |
| Motor auto-slowed in Phase A | Biga pieces too large | Tear smaller; add a bit more of the water up front |
| Dough over DDT | FF underestimated | Raise your FF; colder water next time |
| Dough under DDT | FF overestimated, or water off target | Lower FF; extend Phase C ~1 min per °F, to 5.5 min max |
| Dough tight, springs back on opening | Over-mixed in Phase C, or under-tempered | Cut Phase C by 1 min; check ball core is 60–65 °F |
| Dough slack and sticky at open | Over-fermented — likely warm fridge or stacked trays | Check actual fridge temp; spread trays; drop DDT 1–2 °F |
| Balls blown out and bubbly at 72 h | Too much total yeast | Drop biga ADY 15%, or move to Track B |
| No oven spring, dense cornicione | Under-fermented, or launched cold | Longer temper; verify fridge isn't below 36 °F |
| Burn ring at the base of the cornicione | Unsauced, thinnest part of the base | Sauce closer to the rim; gentler opening; brush off loose flour (§9) |
| Pale crust on long ferments | Residual sugars consumed | Shorten the cold ferment. Don't bake longer — it dries the crumb. |

## 12. Building the calculator — what actually needs measuring

**Only one thing blocks you: friction factor.** Everything else in this document is either arithmetic you already have (§3 scaling, §6 water temp) or published data you can cite (§5 yeast). FF is the single input that can only come from your own machine.

### ✅ Bake 1 — 21 Aug 2026, 6 balls

| | |
|---|---|
| Biga | 3:30 PM Thu, 2 h RT + ~19 h fridge, in the mixer bowl |
| Biga at mix | 53 °F at pull, **58 °F after tearing** |
| Room / flour | 70 / 69 °F |
| Water used | 63.0 °F, 352 g — **should have been 68.0 °F** (bowl-free model was wrong by 5 °F) |
| Probe @ 11 min | 67.5 °F |
| Phase C | 14 → 20.5 min (6.5 min @ 30%) |
| **Final dough** | **73.5 °F** — 1.5 °F under, exactly the water error |
| **FF measured** | **14.04 °F** ✅ |
| Bowl | 965 g stainless |

**What it taught us:**
- The **bowl term** is required. Its *mass* is what matters most, but the temperature is not free either — see below
- Phase C rate climbed 0.82 → 1.00 °F/min as the bowl equilibrated. **This is evidence the bowl started colder than the dough**, consistent with the biga gaining 5 °F from tearing while the bowl stayed at pull temperature
- Rest + Phase D gained +0.5 °F, not the −0.14 predicted → rest loss scales with the dough-to-room gap
- **Phase A water was guessed, so the "too dry" observation is confounded.** Now specified in grams. Needs a clean repeat.
- Total motor time 18.5 min vs ~15 nominal

⚠️ **FF = 14.04 was fitted with `T_bowl` assumed equal to 58 °F.** If the bowl was actually nearer the 53 °F pull temperature, the same measurement solves to **FF = 14.58**. Both fits reproduce bake 1 exactly, because the error was absorbed into FF at the batch size it was fitted at — and that is precisely why it will not stay hidden:

| Balls | Water target, bowl-58 fit | Water target, bowl-53 fit | Divergence |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 3 | 73.6 °F | 75.2 °F | **+1.67** |
| 6 | 68.0 °F | 68.0 °F | +0.03 |
| 9 | 62.9 °F | 62.4 °F | −0.52 |
| 18 | 61.1 °F | 60.1 °F | −1.07 |

**Next:** run 3 balls and 9 balls. If the bowl model is right, FF stays near 14 while the raw rise differs (11.5 vs 13.0).

**Measure the bowl on both** — one reading against the bowl wall at mix start. Without it the 3-ball bake cannot be read cleanly: a bowl-temperature error and a wrong FF are the same 1.7 °F, pointing the same direction, and there is no way to tell them apart after the fact. This is the one measurement that turns bake 2 from suggestive into decisive.

### Tier 1 — blocking

| Measure | How | Reps |
|---|---|---|
| **FF at 3, 6, 9 balls** | Log all inputs, run §8 exactly, probe immediately, `FF = measured − predicted` | 2–3 each |
| **Tap water temp** | Thermometer. Make it a form field, not a constant — it swings seasonally | ongoing |
| **Bowl mass** | Kitchen scale, once | ✅ 965 g → C = 115.8 |
| **FF at 6 balls** | Bake 1 | ✅ 14.04 °F |
| **FF at 3 and 9 balls** | The falsifiable test of the bowl model | ⬜ next |
| **Actual fridge temp** | Probe on the shelf you use, not the dial | once, then spot-check |

One rep gets you most of the value. Three gets you a mean and a sense of the spread, which tells you how tight your DDT can realistically be.

### Tier 2 — where you beat Rise

**FF is not one number, and treating it as one is exactly what fixed-FF calculators get wrong.**

- **It scales with batch size.** More mass means more total work and less surface area per unit mass to shed it. Expect 9-ball to run several degrees above 3-ball.
- **It drifts with room temperature.** Heat lost to the room during a 15-minute mix scales with the gap between the dough and the air. The same profile in a 78 °F kitchen yields a higher effective FF than in a 64 °F one.

So log **room temperature on every single mix.** After 8–10 logged batches, regress `FF = a + b × (T_room − 70)` per batch size. That's a model no generic calculator has, and it's the honest reason to build your own.

**Also worth one afternoon:** probe a ball core every 30 min after it goes in the fridge, once, spread and once stacked. That gives you the real time-above-50 °F budget instead of my estimate. Home Assistant could log this continuously and turn it into a curve.

### Tier 3 — the yeast model (do last, or don't)

Fitting your own fermentation curve is far harder than FF, because you get **one data point per batch** and ripeness is a judgment call.

Make it objective first: fill a small straight-sided jar with biga from the same batch, mark the start level, and read percent rise off the glass. Now "~20% puffed" is a number.

Then log yeast %, the actual temperature profile, and hours to +20%. But be realistic — **adopt PizzaBlab's curve and fit a single multiplier to it.** One correction factor from ten batches beats a curve you fit from scratch on thirty.
